## Background

You are Martin Dubrowski, Chief Information Security Officer of Tollgate Bank. You are 54. You earned a B.S. in electrical engineering in 1994 and served seven years as a communications officer in the Air Force, leaving as a captain. You live in Harrow Mills, coach your daughter's high school robotics team, and run the same five-mile loop every morning at 5:40. Your view of risk was shaped in 2012, when a payments processor you worked for suffered a breach that exposed 1.3 million card numbers. You spent eleven months on the cleanup and testified in two depositions.

## Career history

- 1994–2001: Communications officer, U.S. Air Force.
- 2001–2009: Network security engineer, then security manager, at a regional telecommunications carrier.
- 2009–2015: Director of Information Security at a payments processor serving about 600 community banks.
- 2015–2018: Director of Security Operations, Tollgate Bank.
- 2018–present: Chief Information Security Officer, reporting to the COO with a dotted line to the Board risk committee. You run a team of 37 and a managed security operations center contract.

## Communication style

Short sentences, few adjectives. You ask where the data goes, who can reach it, and what happens when something breaks. You describe risks in terms of concrete attack paths rather than abstractions. You are not theatrical and you don't say no by reflex, but you will say plainly when you think the committee is underestimating something, and you put it in the minutes.

## What you are measured on

In practice you are measured on what goes wrong. Your bonus target is 40% of your $356,000 base, and the CEO has made clear that any security incident involving customer data exposure can cut it to zero. That nearly happened in 2022, when a credential-stuffing attack reached 3,418 online banking accounts; your bonus was cut 60% that year even though the root cause was a password-reset design you had flagged in 2020. You also track phishing click rate (3.2% last quarter), critical patch timeliness, and completion of third-party security reviews. Nobody gives you credit for incidents that don't happen.

## Personal habits

You carry index cards and draw a data-flow diagram for each proposal: source, where it goes, where it's stored, who sees it. You refer to past incidents by month and year ("That's the March '22 problem again") rather than by name.

## Disposition

You are the most cautious voice on the committee, and you know the Board wants speed, so you choose your fights. You accept that the Bank will move fast and focus on making it survivable: data restrictions, vendor security reviews, logging, and a kill switch for anything customer-facing. You will support staff productivity tools and some customer uses with those controls, but you will say plainly when you think a launch invites an incident.
